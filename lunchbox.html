How the foods work together

  • Pulses + lemon. Plant iron sits beside an acidic fruit that contains vitamin C — the same question as our lentils + bell pepper pairing. Not measured for this box.
  • Sweetness from fruit. The sweet part of the box is banana and dates, flavoured with a whole cinnamon stick.
  • Packed for the morning. Wet and dry foods are in separate containers, the bread is wrapped on its own, and the tomato and cucumber travel whole.

How the foods work together

  • One tomato roasted, one raw. Vitamin C is sensitive to heat, so the same food arrives in two different states. Comparing raw and cooked versions is built into how our pairings are measured.
  • Two starchy foods. Mash and bread in one box. Our planned starch-breakdown measurement compares combinations like this; nothing is measured yet.
  • Autumn fruit, whole. Persimmon and apple need no packing and no preparation.
  • A spoon for the mash. A small practical detail that decides whether a box can actually be eaten at a school desk.

How the foods work together

  • Grilled meat + fresh herbs. Two forms of iron in one meal — haem from the meat, non-haem from the greens. This is the question behind our planned beef + leafy greens pairing.
  • Traditional flavours. Sumac onions and yellow-and-white rice come from family cooking. Read the archive records for kabab with sumac onions and plov.
  • Salad, not whole vegetables. Unlike Boxes 01 and 02, the vegetables arrive chopped and ready to eat, on top of the rice.
  • A snack built in. Nuts and raisins in their own glass. Any box with nuts needs checking against allergies and school rules before it is shared.

How we put a lunchbox together

Practical first, then scientific. A school lunch has to survive a morning in a bag, be eaten at a desk, and be affordable — before any of its chemistry matters.

  1. Use everyday food

    Bread, tomatoes, cucumbers, rice, chicken, seasonal fruit — food families already buy.

    Seen in our boxes

  2. Cover the food groups

    A starchy food, a protein food, and fruit or vegetables in every box.

    Seen in our boxes

  3. Pack for the morning

    Wet and dry apart, bread wrapped, fruit and vegetables whole. Cooked meat and dairy kept cool.

    Seen in our boxes

  4. Take questions to the bench

    Combinations in a box become questions for the lab — pulses with lemon, raw versus roasted tomato.

    Pairings in preparation

  5. See what comes back

    What is eaten, and what comes home uneaten. That decides whether a box worked.

    Planned — needs consent

Designing for affordability

A good box is only useful if families can buy it

The Azerbaijan School Lunch Atlas is planned as a record of what is genuinely available to schoolchildren here: what the shops stock near schools, what it costs in manat, when each ingredient is in season, which are produced locally, what survives a morning in a lunchbox, and how much of it comes home again uneaten.

Price points as a design constraint

The project sets itself three budgets to design against. These are targets the team has chosen to keep the work honest about affordability — not menus that have been costed or tested.

  • 3 AZN per lunch
  • 5 AZN per lunch
  • 7 AZN per lunch

No lunchbox has been priced on this site. Costs will appear only once they have been collected from named shops on a recorded date.

Planned tool — not yet built

LunchBox AI

The intended end point of the dataset: enter an age, a budget, the length of the school day, allergies and the foods available, and the program searches combinations from the project's own measurements — balancing nutritional quality and cost against what students actually finish rather than leave.

It does not exist yet. This site will not display sample recommendations before the tool is built and validated, because a demonstration output is indistinguishable from a real one once it is on a screen. The Food Library shows only what our own boxes contain.
