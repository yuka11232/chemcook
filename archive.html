Strand 03 · Cultural knowledge

Intergenerational Food Archive

Grandparents know things about food that no lab manual records: how to keep yogurt from splitting, why bread is never thrown away, which herbs belong on the table. This archive connects that knowledge to the lunchboxes we pack today.

Where the archive stands

The team has recorded 25 family recipes — Azerbaijani, Caspian and Mediterranean — through conversations with parents and grandparents. They are being added here, each checked with the family first. Seven reference records are already open, each linked to our lunchboxes; the interview guide below is how every recipe was collected.

Reference sections are general, widely documented background, marked ○. They are not recipes, and not our research.

Every record has three layers, kept apart

  1. ○

    The tradition

    What the dish is, where it comes from, how it is usually made. General reference, clearly marked.

  2. ▫

    The family

    One family's own version, recorded by interview with permission — in their words, with their amounts.

  3. ●

    The lunchbox

    How the dish or its ingredients show up in what we pack for school today — or could.

    What old kitchens can teach a lunchbox

    Traditional food was designed for the same problems a school lunch has: no fridge, a long wait before eating, and nothing to waste. Each lesson below is visible in a box we have already packed — or suggests a new one.

    1. Sour, without the liquid

      Sumac gives onions their sourness without a dressing that would soak the box.

      Kabab · Box 03

    2. Keep it whole until lunch

      Vegetables chopped just before eating stay crisp. Whole tomato and cucumber travel best.

      Salad · Boxes 01, 02

    3. Stored for winter

      Nuts and dried fruit were made to keep without cold. They make a snack that survives any school day.

      Nuts & dried fruit · Box 03

    4. Nothing wasted

      The custom of never throwing bread away is the same question as our plan to record what comes home uneaten.

      Çörək · Box 02

    5. Hot or cold

      Dovğa is eaten warm in winter and chilled in summer — a starting idea for a yogurt box that has not been made or tested yet.

      Dovğa · Idea

    How a family recipe enters the archive

    The archive is meant to be filled by students talking with the older people in their families. Here is how a record is made — and the questions to start with.

    1. Ask first

      Explain what the archive is and ask permission to record. Agree whether their name is used — or just "a grandmother of a team member".

    2. Listen and write it down

      Use the questions below. Write amounts the way they are given — "a glass of rice", "a handful of dill" — and don't convert them.

    3. Cook it together

      If possible, make the dish with them and photograph it the way our lunchboxes are photographed: overhead, as it is.

    4. Read it back

      The family checks the record before it is published. Then it gets its own layer on the card, marked as collected, with the date.

    Interview guide

    1. What dish do you most connect with your childhood?
    2. Who made it, and who taught them?
    3. What went into it — and where did the ingredients come from?
    4. How much of each? Use the measures you use: glasses, spoons, handfuls.
    5. What is one step a recipe book would leave out?
    6. When was it eaten — every day, in one season, or at celebrations?
    7. How was food kept fresh before fridges were common?
    8. What did children take with them to school, or to the fields?
    9. What was never thrown away, and what happened to leftovers?
    10. How has the dish changed between your generation and ours?

    Recipes, names and photographs are published only with the agreement of the person who shared them.
